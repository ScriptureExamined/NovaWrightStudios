# The Indie Visibility Problem

## (And Why Marketing Starts on Day One)

Every year, more games get made. Every year, fewer of them get seen.

It's not that quality has dropped. If anything, the average indie game today is more polished than ever. The problem is exposure:

- storefronts are flooded with new releases every single day
- players have more choices than they have time
- algorithms reward existing momentum, not new arrivals

A great game with no visibility is functionally the same as a game that doesn't exist.

---

## Marketing Isn't a Phase, It's a Foundation

Many developers treat marketing as something that happens after development: a trailer here, a store page there, maybe a launch week push.

But by the time a game is finished, most of the window to build an audience has already closed.

Visibility has to be built alongside the game, not bolted on afterward:

- a following takes time to grow
- trust takes repeated exposure to earn
- momentum compounds, it doesn't appear overnight

Waiting until launch to start talking about your game is like planting a tree the week you want fruit.

---

## Wishlist Culture Has Changed the Timeline

Storefront algorithms now weigh pre-launch signals heavily. Wishlists, followers, and early community engagement all feed into how visible a game becomes at launch.

This changes the shape of development itself:

- developers now share early builds, not just finished trailers
- devlogs and progress updates matter as much as marketing copy
- a quiet development cycle can quietly doom a launch

The work of getting noticed now starts long before the work of finishing the game ends.

---

## The Discoverability Paradox

There's a frustrating irony at the heart of indie visibility:

To get noticed, you need an audience. To build an audience, you need to be noticed.

Breaking that cycle usually comes down to a few things:

- a clear, specific hook that's easy to describe in one sentence
- consistency, showing up regularly instead of in bursts
- authenticity, sharing real process instead of polished-only moments

Players don't just wishlist games. They follow developers whose work they trust to keep showing up.

---

## A Lesson From Project Echo

Writing this weekly devlog isn't just documentation, it's the visibility strategy.

Project Echo has a hook that's easy to say out loud: time manipulation as the core mechanic. But an easy hook doesn't market itself. It still has to reach people, over and over, before it sticks.

So each post tries to do two things at once:

- share something genuinely useful about the design process
- show enough of the game that the hook stays visible

The goal isn't to promote the game every week. It's to make sure that when the game is ready, the audience already exists.

---

## Quality Still Matters, It's Just Not Sufficient

None of this means marketing replaces good design. A great marketing push for a mediocre game just gets more people to bounce off it faster.

But the reverse is just as true:

- a brilliant game nobody hears about sells nothing
- a good game with a real audience can outperform a great game with none

Visibility and quality aren't competing priorities. They're both required, and increasingly, they have to be built at the same time.

---

## Final Thought

The indie market isn't shrinking. It's growing, and that's exactly the problem.

More games means more competition for the same finite attention. Standing out isn't about being loudest, it's about being visible early, consistently, and honestly.

Because the best time to start building an audience for your game:

**was the day you started making it.**
