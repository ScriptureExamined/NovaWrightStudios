# Your Store Page Is Your First Level

## (How an Indie Developer's Storefront Is a Design Problem in Disguise)

Most indie developers spend months — sometimes years — designing levels, tuning mechanics, and polishing the moment-to-moment feel of their game. Then, sometime close to launch, they write a store description, upload a few screenshots, and call it done.

I understand why. Writing copy and selecting images feels like the boring part. It's not the game itself. It's the packaging.

But here's what I've come to believe: the store page is a level. It has a goal (conversion), a path the visitor follows (the scroll), obstacles to overcome (confusion, skepticism, indifference), and a moment where the visitor either commits or walks away. If you have never thought about it in those terms, your store page is probably underperforming — not because your game isn't good enough, but because the page isn't doing its job.

---

## The Store Page Has a Job

Most players will make their decision to buy — or not — within the first few seconds of arriving at a store page. That window is smaller than the first room of almost any game you've ever designed.

In that tiny amount of time, the page has to communicate: what kind of game is this, who is it for, and does it look worth playing?

It does not have to explain every mechanic. It does not need to cover the entire storyline. It does not need to show off everything the developer is proud of. It needs to make someone feel something specific — curiosity, excitement, recognition — quickly enough that they keep reading or watching instead of clicking away.

That's a design problem. It requires the same clarity that good level design requires. Too much information too fast and the visitor gets overwhelmed. Too little and the visitor doesn't understand why they should care.

---

## Screenshots Are the First Puzzle

On most platforms, the screenshot strip appears before anything else — before the description, before the trailer, before the player has read a single word about the game. Six to eight images that are supposed to communicate the entire experience.

A lot of developers treat screenshots as documentation. Here is what the game looks like. Here is the interface. Here is the character. Here is a level.

That approach misses the point. Screenshots are advertisements for a specific feeling. They should show the most visually compelling moments. The shots that communicate the game's personality, not its feature list. The images that make someone think: I want to be in that world, or I want to figure out how that works, or that looks different from anything I've seen.

Think of the first few screenshots as the hook — the same function a game's opening moments serve. If the screenshot strip doesn't create some kind of desire or question in the visitor, the rest of the page is fighting a losing battle.

---

## The Description Is a Tutorial

A store page description has roughly the same challenge as a game tutorial: it needs to communicate essential information to someone who doesn't know anything about what they're looking at, without losing them before they get to the part that matters.

The instinct is to list features. "40 handcrafted levels. A unique time-bending mechanic. Atmospheric sound design." These things might all be true, but a feature list doesn't create the desire to play. It just informs. Information alone rarely converts.

What works better is communicating the experience — what it feels like to play the game, what problem or feeling the player is chasing, what moment they're working toward. A description that makes someone think "I know exactly what this feels like to play" is doing more work than a description that just lists what the game contains.

Keep the most important information early. Put the hook in the first two sentences, because many platforms truncate descriptions with a "read more" fold, and a large percentage of visitors never unfold it.

---

## The Capsule Art Is the First Impression

The capsule image is what players see before they reach the store page at all — in search results, in recommendations, in the "More like this" sections that account for an enormous amount of discovery on platforms like Steam.

A capsule image has maybe half a second to stop a scroll. It isn't supposed to explain the game. It's supposed to make someone curious enough to click.

Clear, high-contrast images tend to outperform busy ones. A single central subject is usually stronger than a collage. A distinct visual identity — something that looks different from adjacent games in the same genre — does more work than a technically impressive image that blends into a crowded store.

The capsule art should be designed last, after the developer has a clear sense of what the game is actually about — not what it contains, but what it *is*.

---

## What NovaWright Is Learning

I'm in the process of thinking more seriously about how to present Project Echo and Number Push on their eventual store pages. The natural instinct is to explain the mechanics, because the mechanics are the interesting part from a developer's perspective. Time manipulation. Past and Present. Switching between two versions of a world to solve puzzles.

But from a player's perspective, the interesting part isn't the mechanic. It's the feeling the mechanic creates — the moment where you realize the past version of a room holds the answer to the present one, and everything clicks. That's the thing the store page needs to communicate. Not how it works. How it feels.

Getting that distinction right is something I'm still working on. It requires thinking less like the person who made the game and more like someone who has never heard of it.

---

## Final Thought

A store page isn't something you make after the game is finished. It's something you develop alongside the game — because the process of describing your game to someone who has never seen it is one of the most useful things a developer can do. If you can't explain what makes your game feel different in two sentences, that's worth knowing long before launch.

**The game deserves a storefront that works as hard as the game itself does.**
